During college, I fell in love hard with computer science. I spent a long time reading computer science books, blogs, talks and basically building a catalogue of CS books I’ve read and want to read. This also spilled over to my coding exercises and projects, even though I don’t have many projects (and most of my projects were kind of “throwaway” ones where I deleted them from my GitHub because they sucked), I really liked to experiment with different languages and styles of projects. Things died down after I started working full time and finished college, because most of my personal time these last few years was spent hanging out with my friends, playing video games and guitar. 

After a while, I decided that I wanted to become an amazing programmer, especially in databases and distributed systems. So I devised a plan with projects and challenges I needed to do to be able to achieve my goal (you can look through my last post if you want see my plan). It covered a lot of topics in a lot of different languages, and I could see myself having fun with this stuff and growing as a software engineer/programmer. The plan was to do all these projects without any AI help to write the code, and if I had a very specific question that would be more easily answered by an AI, I would ask the question like one human being asking another, and never ask the AI to implement anything for me. My goal was to do everything by hand, and only use AI for answering specific questions.

A few weeks ago, I talked to another software engineer from a company other than my own, and I asked them about the AI culture in their current company, how they and their colleagues use it, and how AI is integrated into their workflows. Their company-wide culture is to use AI agents as the main editors of code, you would decide what needs to be done, then offload the request to some AI agent that would edit the code and do anything else that needed to be done. and honestly, I can’t fault them, in the past few months, I have been doing this too. The productivity gains are immense, if I had to build or implement something in my job, I would spend a good chunk of my time figuring out how to do it within my current constraints, like the technologies and languages we use, how to deploy it, how to integrate it with the rest of the code, or even how to actually do it with what has already been built. I can circumvent all of this work just by asking a capable AI agent to implement it for me, reviewing it to see if it’s what I want, committing , deploying, and continuing the rest of my work. 

So I sat down and started thinking about a question that has been probably asked a million times, which is “should I focus my free time actually programming and learning new stuff, or should I spend my free time practicing things where the only immediate advantage is helping me ace a technical interview, like leetcode or system design?”. The answer back then was to strike a balance, or prioritize one or the other depending on your current circumstances. Because well, if you prioritize the “interview skills” and get the job, you would be lost, wouldn’t know what you were doing, your performance would be horrible, and you probably would have been fired. It’s not realistic to say that if you focused more on projects you would suck at solving the kind of questions that appear in common tech (leetcode-y) interviews, but being able to do them in ~40 minutes tops while explaining your thought process? That needs a lot of practice. Same for system design. You would move the slider based on your current life: if you are looking for a job, spend more time practicing the “interview skills”, if you are employed, the projects that you did on the job would account for the “programming” itch, and you didn’t need to worry about having impeccable interview skills anyway, so your free time would be available to do more “project programming”.

But now, we have these amazing AI agents that have incredibly vast knowledge, and even though some people say they suck at programming and need to be babysat constantly, they are still amazing programmers that will build something for you, if you have the time to set the guardrails, like tests, specs and more. One year and a half ago, AI was just a fancier auto-complete and web browser chat that would sometimes say a bunch of bullshit, but it would actually hit the mark on some other occasions. Now we have super models that can basically do anything coding-wise. It’s really scary how fast the models are progressing. 

Even though there is a financial cost to this, and I have seen cases where companies have cautioned some engineers to go easy on their token usage because of money, I do believe that this is a revolution in the way that we write code and that it honestly might stay in the long term. So here is my updated question: For my career, is it even worth it to spend so much time with these super complex projects and actually knowing how to implement these things, instead of focusing 100% on the skills that will help me land a job, where I will, with a 99% certainty, use AI to do the actual work? Or in other words, should I be a good programmer, or should I practice the skills that make me look like a good programmer in an interview now that AI basically accounts for the “not knowing what you are doing” issue now that you can ask it to do it for you?

I don’t have an answer to that question. I definitely love programming, that’s the reason why I built this plan in the first place, but I keep thinking about my career and where I want to be, and what I need to do to get there, and I don’t know if this plan will help me get there.
